# Three Horizons

**The short version:** Map three patterns that exist at the same time: today's system, which is losing fit (H1); the emerging successor, already visible in pockets (H3); and the turbulent transition between them (H2). Then sort your innovations into those that lead somewhere new (H2+) and those that just prop up the old system (H2−).

---

## Who made it

The futures version of Three Horizons was developed by Bill Sharpe and Anthony Hodgson, starting with the UK Government Foresight project on Intelligent Infrastructure Systems (2006), and practised through the International Futures Forum. Curry & Hodgson (2008) set out its theory and history. Sharpe et al. (2016) describe it as a practice for transformation, drawing on about 180 uses.

**Watch out for the name.** The first published "Three Horizons" is a McKinsey management model from *The Alchemy of Growth* (1999): short-, medium- and long-term growth businesses drawn as successive curves. The futures practice is a different thing. Its horizons exist **at the same time**, and they describe patterns in a system, not a portfolio of growth bets. We expect AI tools to mix the two up (a hypothesis the fdbx baseline probe will test), so the skill is explicit about which one it uses.

---

## The problem it solves

Organisations facing change tend to split into two camps. People who keep today's system running see the visionaries as unrealistic. The visionaries see the operators as blockers. Meanwhile there is lots of "innovation", and nobody can tell whether it leads anywhere new or just makes the old system slightly better at being the old system.

Three Horizons gives both camps a shared map in minutes. It shows that each role matters: without H1 nothing gets resourced, and without H3 H1 eventually collapses.

---

## When to use it

- The current model is visibly losing fit and you need a transition, not just a destination
- You have lots of innovation activity and can't tell which of it matters
- Operators and visionaries are talking past each other
- You have a vision (for example from Four Futures) and need a pathway from here
- At the start of a longer project, as a quick shared frame

---

## What it produces

- **The map:** H1, H2, H3, each with evidence and who holds it
- **H1 losing fit,** including "fixes that fail", where improvements deepen the underlying problem
- **H3 aspirations** (more than one) and **pockets of the future** already existing today, each in the Evidence Ledger
- **H2 innovations,** each marked H2+ or H2− with a reason
- **Features to maintain** from H1
- **A dilemma** between an H1 value and an H3 value, and who has the power to resist or enable change
- **Horizon perspectives:** how each horizon sees the others, as a fixed mindset and as a positive perspective
- **Actions** tied to horizons, and, if you list them, a **classification of your current initiatives**

---

## The key insight

Not all innovation is transformative. An H2 innovation that makes the old system faster or cheaper is often H2−: it gets absorbed and extends H1's life. Asking of every initiative "does this open space for H3, or does it prop up H1?" changes portfolio conversations.

The second insight is about people. Once the operator can see the visionary as "hope for renewal" instead of "fantasy", and the visionary can see the operator as "the skills we'll need to scale" instead of "the dinosaur", the conversation becomes about managing a transition together.

---

## How to use it with AI

Name the area of concern, a horizon length suited to the domain, who is involved, any evidence of stress or of emerging alternatives, and your current initiatives if you want them classified. The skill produces the map, the H2+/− classification, features to keep, a dilemma, the horizon perspectives, actions, an Evidence Ledger, and a suggested next method.

The practice's authors say facilitator skill strongly affects how deep a session goes. Treat the AI output as preparation for a conversation, not a substitute for one.
