# Causal Layered Analysis

**The short version:** Read a problem at four depths, from the headline version down to the story people carry, then climb back up with a different story and see what brief it produces.

---

## Who made it

Sohail Inayatullah introduced Causal Layered Analysis in a 1998 article in *Futures*, building on Richard Slaughter's idea that futures work can be shallow or deep. He first presented it at a World Futures Studies Federation course in Bangkok in 1993. In his 2008 "Six Pillars" paper it is the core of the *deepening* pillar.

---

## The problem it solves

Most briefs are written at the surface. "Users feel disconnected; design a feature that increases connection, measured by weekly active use." The problem, the solution and the success metric all arrive together, and the design team's job looks like execution.

CLA's premise is that how you frame a problem decides which solutions look possible and who is expected to deliver them. A team that never questions the frame can only produce better versions of the solution already in the brief.

---

## When to use it

- A brief arrives with its solution already inside it
- The team keeps generating variations of the same idea
- Stakeholders seem to be arguing about different problems
- Before any scenario work, so that the scenarios differ in depth and not only in degree
- A problem has been "fixed" several times and keeps coming back

---

## What it produces

| Layer | What you write | Who acts here |
|---|---|---|
| **Litany** | The problem as officially described: headlines, metrics, the brief itself | Someone else: government, management, "the platform" |
| **Social causes** | The economic, political, technological, historical explanation | Partnerships and institutions |
| **Discourse / worldview** | The way of seeing that makes this *the* problem, plus at least one rival way of seeing | People and voluntary associations |
| **Myth / metaphor** | The deep story, as an image or a line | Leaders and artists |

Then the skill climbs back up from an **alternative metaphor** and rewrites the brief. It also gives one scenario per layer and an Evidence Ledger, so you can see which claims came from you, which from a source, and which the model assumed.

---

## The key insight

The deeper layers are not more "true" than the litany. Every layer is real and needs its own response. What CLA shows is that most organisations only ever act at the top two layers, so their solutions keep reproducing the worldview that created the problem.

The second insight is the climb back up. Stopping at "the real problem is our worldview" is a well-known failure; Inayatullah names paralysis as the method's main risk. The value is in the reframed brief you can put next to the original.

---

## Worked example (illustrative, not from the source)

**Brief (litany):** "Remote employees feel disconnected. Design a team space that increases weekly active use."

- **Social causes:** distributed teams, meeting overload, fewer informal moments, managers measured on output.
- **Worldview:** two discourses. *Engagement*: connection is a product usage problem. *Belonging*: connection comes from shared purpose and trust, which a tool can support but not create.
- **Myth / metaphor:** "The office is where the team lives." Alternative: "The team is a crew, not a place."
- **Back up:** under the crew metaphor, the brief becomes "Help teams keep the rituals that make them a crew, wherever they are. Success means team members say they know who to ask for help", not weekly active use.

---

## How to use it with AI

Give the brief in the organisation's own words, who wrote it, and the context, including communities whose view of the issue might differ. The skill fills the four layers, names at least two worldviews, proposes an alternative metaphor, rebuilds a reframed brief, writes one scenario per layer, and ends with an Evidence Ledger and a suggested next method.
