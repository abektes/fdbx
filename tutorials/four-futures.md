# Four Futures

**The short version:** Put your product or service into four fundamentally different futures (Continued Growth, Collapse, Discipline, Transformation), take each one seriously, and find what to do now, including the design decisions that hold in all four.

---

## Who made it

Jim Dator, of the Hawaii Research Center for Futures Studies at the University of Hawaii at Manoa (the "Manoa School" of futures studies), developed the four generic futures. Inayatullah dates the model to Dator's 1979 work. Dator's 2009 article "Alternative Futures at the Manoa School" (*Journal of Futures Studies*) explains the model and how to run it as a workshop.

---

## The problem it solves

Ask a team about the future and it will usually describe the present, extended and amplified. Business cases and roadmaps usually contain one future, and it is usually growth. Dator calls Continued Growth the official view of modern institutions.

Dator's answer is not better prediction. He says the future can't be predicted. Instead he makes people *experience* futures different enough from the present that the one-future assumption breaks. Having lived for an hour in a disciplined, low-growth society, a team can no longer treat growth as the only realistic option.

---

## When to use it

- The roadmap or business case has exactly one future in it
- The team calls one scenario "realistic" and the others "edge cases"
- You want to stress-test a concept against very different worlds
- You are preparing a futures workshop and need the four future handouts
- Before a vision exercise, so the vision is chosen rather than defaulted to

---

## What it produces

| Future | The belief underneath |
|---|---|
| **Continued Growth** | Keep the economy growing; technology solves problems. The official view of most institutions. |
| **Collapse** | Something breaks: economy, environment, society. Not the worst case: some people welcome it, and there are winners. |
| **Discipline** | Growth is unsustainable or undesirable; life is organised around survival, fairness and shared values. |
| **Transformation** | Technology remakes life, and humanity with it. |

For each future: a description written from inside and positively, an artifact from that future, and answers to Dator's five questions (life there, probability, preferability, five things to do now toward it, five things to do now against it), plus how your product would thrive there. Across all four: the **robust design decisions**, the **single-future bets**, and a signal to watch for each future.

In **workshop kit** mode you get four separate handouts and a facilitation and debrief guide instead.

---

## The key insight

The rules are the method. **No best case, no worst case, no most likely case.** All four are equally probable in the long run and are presented positively, including Collapse. That feels wrong at first, and it is the point. As soon as one future is labelled "most likely", the other three become decoration and the exercise collapses back into planning for one future.

A second insight: the four are not four settings of one dial. Each rests on a different belief about how the world works. "Fast adoption" and "slow adoption" are one future at two speeds.

---

## A note on names

Dator calls the third future **Discipline**. Sohail Inayatullah, summarising Dator's model in 2008, calls it **Steady state**, and adds that Transformation can be spiritual as well as technological. The skill uses Dator's names and labels Inayatullah's additions.

---

## How to use it with AI

Name the subject, a horizon (Dator's default is 20–50 years), the relevant history and present conditions, and whether you want the analysis or a workshop kit. The skill writes four futures, experiences each with Dator's questions, places your product in each, and ends with the design decisions that hold in all four, an Evidence Ledger, and a suggested next method.
