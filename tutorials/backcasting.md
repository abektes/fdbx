# Backcasting

**The short version:** Stand in the future you want, ask what had to happen to get there, then what had to happen before that, all the way back to today. Score each critical step by how much of it you control, name who loses out, and leave with an action plan.

---

## Who made it

Sohail Inayatullah credits the technique to the peace researcher Elise Boulding, who moved people into a preferred future and asked for their memories of how it came about (*Six Pillars*, 2008). The version in this skill follows the UK Government Office for Science *Futures Toolkit* (2nd edition, 2024), which sets it out as seven workshop steps. In energy policy, the term is usually traced to John Robinson's 1982 paper; fdbx names that paper but has not checked it.

---

## The problem it solves

Most roadmaps are built forward. Start with what the team is doing now, extend it, and stop where the plan runs out, usually well short of the goal. The toolkit calls the pull behind this **present bias**: we prefer immediate gains to long-term ones.

Backcasting reverses the direction. Starting from the end state, every gap between it and today has to be filled, including the parts that are not the team's to fill.

---

## When to use it

- You have a goal with a year on it, and the roadmap stops long before it
- A preferred future came out of a Four Futures or visioning session, and nobody has worked out the path
- Everything outside the team's control is listed as a "dependency" and left there
- You want to test whether a goal is achievable before committing to it
- You want to see how a feared future could arrive, so you can block it

---

## What it produces

- **The preferred future**, described as what people can do there, what is true, and what is no longer true
- **Key differences** between now and then, across political, economic, societal, technological, legislative and environmental areas
- **Memories from the future**: short first-person accounts of how it happened
- **A backward timeline**, where every step is what had to happen before the one after it
- **Control scores** for each critical event: wholly, partly, or not in our control
- For events in our control: impact, **who benefits and who loses out**, certainty, enablers, barriers, steps now
- For events outside our control: **who has control**, what happens if the event doesn't, and what we can influence
- **An action plan** with owners and decision points
- **Unknowns to fill**: the present-day facts the plan depends on that you haven't supplied

---

## The key insight

The control score changes the plan. A forward roadmap quietly drops what the team can't do. The toolkit says to keep those events on the timeline anyway. For each one, the question becomes *who does control this, and what can we do to make it more likely?* That is usually where the real work is.

The second insight is the toolkit's question about losers, including people who *may feel* they will lose out. A plan in which nobody loses has not been looked at closely.

---

## How to use it with AI

Give the goal and its year, what you know about where things stand now (including any figures), and who is involved. The skill will not invent your current figures: a plain AI model tends to ("60% of your buses are low-floor"), so this skill lists what it needs as questions instead. It works backwards, scores control, and ends with an action plan, an Evidence Ledger and a suggested next method. Ask for *avoid* mode to backcast a future you want to prevent.
