# Scanning as a team routine

The GO-Science *Futures Toolkit* (pp. 36–41) describes horizon scanning both
as a one-off project and as a continuous function, where it becomes part of
people's jobs to log interesting developments. This is its setup, with fdbx
notes for design teams marked as adaptations.

## The group

- Start with **no more than 10 scanners**; widen the network later.
- Aim for **diversity**: different backgrounds, and people from outside the organisation if you can.
- A small project team (2–3 people) organises the scan. One person can do it alone, but must then consult widely to avoid a narrow view.

## The rhythm

- Each scanner writes **one short scan per week**: what it is about, how it relates to the scoping question, and why they think it matters. The toolkit's example: 10 people over 6 weeks give 60 or more scans.
- Everyone uses the same format and metadata (date, author, source link, PESTLE category, first view of timescale and importance). See the Scans table in `SKILL.md`.
- The project team gathers the scans and keeps the PESTLE count, so gaps show early.

## The workshop

Bring the scanners together to review the scans: cluster them into themes that emerge (the natural agenda), type the signals, place them on the impact and likelihood matrix, and choose four to eight drivers. Then write up. If scanning continues, use the workshop's output to focus the next round.

## Notes for design teams

> **fdbx adaptation.**

- Keep a shared "signals" channel or board where anyone can drop a link with one line on why it matters. The weekly scan is written from it.
- Include signals from research and support: what users do that the product did not expect is often the earliest signal a design team has. Record it as `user-supplied` with where it was observed.
- When an AI assistant helps, give it the channel's contents and use this skill's *analyse* mode. Treat its leads to verify as a to-do list, not as findings.
